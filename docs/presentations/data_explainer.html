Step 1

The basics: one row = one supplier

Each of the 10,000 rows is a company that sells parts or services to the railway operator. Each row has three kinds of information:

18 risk scores
financial, quality, ESG, cyber… → 1 total risk score
a weighted average + Business numbers
spend, delivery, order behaviour

Reading a score: higher = worse

Every score runs from 0 (no risk) to 100 (maximum risk), and each score gets a label from the same four bands. Careful: this holds even for "ESG Performance" and "Credit Rating". A high number there is bad, which is the opposite of how those words normally sound.

Low0 – 24
Medium25 – 49
High50 – 74
Critical75 – 100
Data quality: the file is clean, with no missing values, no duplicates, and every label matching its score. You can trust the numbers as given. Keep in mind that the data is synthetic: supplier names are made up and there is no country, product category or history.

Step 3

How the total risk score is built

We reverse-engineered the formula, and it is exact for all 10,000 suppliers: Total = Σ (weight × score), rounded. The weights add up to 100%. Here is how much each of the 18 scores counts. The colours group related scores into 6 "pillars".

For a procurement engineer: the three heaviest factors, Operational Resilience, Financial Health and Quality (10% each), are exactly the ones that decide whether parts arrive on time and work. A supplier going bankrupt or shipping defective brake parts hurts far more than bad press (Media Sentiment counts only 3%).
Step 4

One supplier, explained point by point

Because the formula is exact, we can show exactly where a supplier's total comes from. Each bar shows how many points a factor adds (weight × score). The bars add up to the total.

Why it matters: you never have to trust a "black box" score. You can say "this supplier is 87 because financial health and credit are at 100", and act on that specific cause, for example by asking for financial guarantees or qualifying a second source.

Key metric: Exposure ★

Exposure = spend × (total risk ÷ 100). It is a rough "euros at risk" number. A €50M supplier with risk 87 has an exposure of €43.5M.

Because spend and risk are unrelated in this data (correlation ≈ 0), exposure adds new information. Big suppliers are not automatically safer or riskier.

Step 7

Delivery performance: what risk looks like on the shop floor

Three operational numbers describe how a supplier behaves day to day. Each chart shows the average per total-risk level.

Delivery Rating ★

% delivered on time and correct. Higher = better. Axis starts at 70%.

Order Confirmation Rate ★

% of orders confirmed in time. Higher = better. Axis starts at 70%.

Order Change Rate ★

% of orders changed after placing. Lower = better.

What this means: Critical suppliers deliver on time only ~80% of the time, against ~100% for Low-risk ones. That is one late delivery in five. The scores that predict late deliveries best are Operational Resilience and Quality, so these are the early-warning signals to watch.
Step 8

How hard is a supplier to replace?

Two internal labels describe this: Sourcing Risk Level ★ (how hard it is to find an alternative) and Supply Chain Complexity ★ (how complicated the logistics and sub-suppliers are). Each cell shows the number of suppliers; darker = more.

For a procurement engineer: a High-risk supplier is a real problem only when you can't switch. "High sourcing risk + Complex" (top-right cell) means single-source, long qualification, and certified rail parts like bogies, brakes or signalling. These suppliers need a backup plan before something goes wrong.
